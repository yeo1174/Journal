The Currency Game: How the Dollar World Quietly Steals from the South

An iPad costs 44 meals in New York. In Kuala Lumpur, it costs 237. The gap is not just about efficiency — it is one of the clearest signs of how the global money system quietly shapes what we pay, what we save, and what we can afford.
By AL
KUALA LUMPUR — Take an Apple iPad Air, the one that costs RM3,799 in Malaysia. Divide it by the price of a standard nasi kandar in downtown KL: roughly RM16. That is 237 meals. Do the same exercise in New York: an iPad Air costs $799. A solid lunch out runs about $18. That is 44 meals.

A Malaysian may work just as hard as anyone else, but the same slab of glass and aluminium asks for far more of her time. Her labour is not worth 20 cents on the dollar. The system she lives under is set up so that it often feels that way.

This is not about the invisible hand of supply and demand. It is about a monetary architecture built at Bretton Woods in 1944, rewritten by the Nixon Shock in 1971, cemented by the petrodollar in 1974, and enforced through the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication system — SWIFT — ever since. Call it the currency game. The rules were not written by a committee of equals. They were written by the winner, for the winner, before most of the losing players even had seats at the table.

The Architecture of Advantage

The United States held two-thirds of the world's gold reserves when 44 Allied nations gathered in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944. The agreement that emerged was straightforward: other nations would peg their currencies to the dollar, and the dollar would be convertible to gold at $35 per ounce. It was the dollar's world. Everybody else just lived in it.

By 1971, the math had broken. The Vietnam War and Great Society spending had flooded the world with dollars, and President Richard Nixon knew that the U.S. did not have enough gold to honor its commitment. On August 15, he unilaterally closed the gold window. The Bretton Woods system collapsed — and with it, any pretense that the global monetary order was a cooperative arrangement rather than a managed one.

What followed was the petrodollar. In 1974, the United States struck a deal with Saudi Arabia: the Saudis would price all oil exports exclusively in dollars, recycle their petrodollar surpluses into U.S. Treasuries, and invest in American debt. In return, the U.S. would guarantee Saudi security. Other OPEC nations fell in line. The result was that anyone who needed oil — which is everyone — needed dollars first.

That requirement is the engine of the currency game. No country can tell the United States to devalue its currency, because the world needs dollars before it can buy energy, and because roughly 88% of all foreign exchange transactions still involve the dollar, and because 57% of global foreign exchange reserves are held in dollars, and because trillions of dollars of cross-border debt are denominated in a currency that no developing country controls.

The Plumbing Is the Policy

The mechanism that makes the system binding is less visible than the petrodollar but arguably more powerful. It is the network of payment and messaging systems — SWIFT, Fedwire, and the Clearing House Interbank Payments System, or CHIPS.

SWIFT is a Belgian cooperative that handles roughly 42 million financial messages per day, connecting 11,000 financial institutions across 200 countries. It does not settle payments; it instructs them. But without SWIFT, a bank in Jakarta cannot reliably tell a bank in New York where to send the money. Removing a country from SWIFT, as the European Union did to seven Russian banks in March 2022, is a financial death sentence.

The Richmond Federal Reserve published a noteworthy paper in March 2022 examining whether SWIFT sanctions could end the dollar's dominance. Its conclusion was candid: market participants cannot switch to non-dollar currencies because their counterparties will not. The network effect is a cage, and the jailer is everyone.

“Payment decisions are made by bilateral economic choice, rather than by coercion,” the Richmond Fed wrote, before acknowledging the catch: the network effect works both ways, but “the anchors of history, social norm and switching cost give the dollar some incumbent advantage.” Some incumbent advantage. The dollar became the world's reserve currency because of Bretton Woods. It stays there because leaving requires a coordinated migration that no single country can accomplish alone. That is not a market. That is a trap.

The Mathematics of Surrender

Malaysians do not need to be told that the exchange rate is unfair. They experience it every time they travel, order a subscription service, or check the price of imported goods. But the unfairness is not a defect of the system. It is the point.

Consider the numbers. Malaysia's GDP per capita in nominal terms was approximately $14,000 in 2025. On a purchasing power parity basis — meaning what that income actually buys inside Malaysia — it was approximately $40,000 combined. The difference between those two numbers is the currency discount, and it amounts to roughly 65%. A Malaysian produces economic value equivalent to $40,000 of Western purchasing power, but the exchange rate says she is worth $14,000. That gap does not disappear. It is captured by the differential between local and global prices.

Everything that crosses a border — energy, electronics, pharmaceuticals, aircraft, shipping, insurance, debt service — is priced in dollars. A Malaysian earning in ringgit must convert her labour into dollars every time she buys something internationally priced. The conversion rate is set by a system she does not control.

What this means in daily life is not abstract. A Malaysian family on the median household income of around RM8,500 a month can rent a decent apartment in a KL suburb for RM2,000. An American family earning the equivalent of roughly RM31,000 a month will pay RM16,000 for a comparable apartment in San Jose. Inside our own economy, the gap in actual purchasing power is often much smaller than the headline exchange rate suggests. But the moment any of us buys something priced internationally — a laptop for the kids, a family holiday abroad, the imported medicine a parent depends on — the gap opens up again. That extra cost is not productivity. It is not skill or effort. It is the currency tax, and Malaysians feel it in ordinary life.

The iPad example is not about Apple’s pricing strategy. It is about what happens when a globally priced good meets locally earned income. The iPad does not cost 5x more in Malaysia. The ringgit buys 5x less on the international market than its real domestic purchasing power would suggest. That gap is the currency tax, and it is extracted every single day on every single transaction that touches the global economy.

Original Sin: Why Developing Countries Cannot Borrow in Their Own Currency

The most insidious feature of the currency game is the debt trap, described by economists Barry Eichengreen, Ricardo Hausmann, and Ugo Panizza as “original sin” in a series of influential papers starting in 1999.

The term refers to a structural fact of global finance: most developing countries cannot borrow abroad in their own currency. A Malaysian company that wants to issue a bond for international investors cannot issue it in ringgit. The investors will not buy it. So the company borrows in dollars.

This creates a permanent mismatch. The company’s revenue is in ringgit. Its debt service is in dollars. If the ringgit falls, the debt does not fall with it. It grows. The company must earn more ringgit to pay the same dollar obligation. Multiply that across every emerging market corporation, every sovereign debt issuance, every infrastructure project financed by international capital, and the scale of the structural disadvantage becomes clear.

Eichengreen and Hausmann demonstrated that original sin was nearly universal among developing economies and that it was independent of a country's history of inflation or currency depreciation. It was not a punishment for bad economic management. It was a feature of the system. Countries that had managed their finances impeccably still could not borrow in their own currencies.

“If a country is unable to borrow abroad in its own currency... then when it accumulates a net debt, it will have an aggregate currency mismatch on its balance sheet.” — Eichengreen, Hausmann & Panizza, “Original Sin” (2003)

The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis was the most dramatic illustration of this vulnerability. Thailand, Indonesia, and South Korea had borrowed heavily in dollars during the boom years of the early 1990s. When their currencies collapsed, their dollar-denominated debts exploded. The IMF’s bailout conditions forced austerity, asset sales, and structural reforms that benefited foreign creditors. The countries that had been fastest-growing in the world were suddenly begging for dollars to survive. The architecture of the system had turned a manageable currency adjustment into a catastrophe.

Malaysia took a different path, and it is a story worth remembering. Then-Prime Minister Mahathir Mohamad rejected the IMF’s conditions outright. On 1 September 1998, Malaysia pegged the ringgit at RM3.80 to the dollar and imposed strict capital controls — preventing speculators from moving money in and out of the country at will. The IMF, Wall Street, and the Western economics establishment condemned the move as reckless and certain to fail. It did not fail. By 2001, Malaysia had recovered faster than Thailand and Indonesia, with less social damage and without surrendering control of its banks and strategic industries to foreign buyers. The lesson was not that Malaysia had found a perfect answer. It was that the rules of the currency game are not natural law. Sometimes the only useful move is to refuse to play by someone else’s script.

The same pattern has repeated in Argentina, Turkey, Sri Lanka, Pakistan, and Zambia. The names change. The mechanics do not.

The Counter-Argument: Is This Really a Conspiracy?

Counterpoint: The dollar’s dominance is not a conspiracy. It is a network effect, and network effects are hard to break. The euro, the yen, and the yuan are all available for trade settlement. No country is forced to use the dollar. Switzerland, Singapore, and the United Arab Emirates have prospered without holding reserve currency status. The real problem is that developing countries have weak institutions, poorly managed fiscal policy, and a history of inflation that makes their currencies unattractive to international investors. “Original sin is not a sin of the system,” one economist put it. “It is a sin of the borrower.” The system did not force Malaysia to run budget deficits, protect crony industries, or tolerate a managed exchange rate. The ringgit's weakness partly reflects domestic policy choices, not global manipulation. Moreover, the dollar system has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty by providing a stable, liquid, and predictable international financial framework. Without it, global trade would be more expensive, more fragmented, and more dangerous. The question is whether the price of stability is a fair one.

The counter-argument has merit. But it also sidesteps a structural truth: no developing country, no matter how well-managed, has ever graduated from original sin. Singapore is the rare exception, and Singapore is a city-state with a sovereign wealth fund larger than most nations' GDP. For every other emerging economy, the currency game is a game they cannot win on merit alone.

The Slow Unraveling

The signs of resistance to the dollar system are accumulating. France repatriated its gold reserves from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York between July 2025 and January 2026, a move that was not publicly criticized but was closely watched by every other allied nation. Canada launched a $25 billion sovereign wealth fund explicitly designed to reduce dependence on the United States. German officials publicly questioned the wisdom of storing 37% of the country’s gold in New York vaults.

Among developing nations, the response has been more direct. ASEAN is advancing local currency settlement agreements that reduce the need for dollar intermediation in intra-regional trade. BRICS expanded from five to eleven members in 2024 and is developing a cross-border payment infrastructure designed to bypass SWIFT. China’s Cross-Border Interbank Payment System, known as CIPS, processed the equivalent of $1.2 trillion in transactions in 2025, up 40% year over year.

The dollar’s share of global foreign exchange reserves has declined from 71% in 1999 to 57% today. That decline has been gradual, not catastrophic, but it has been consistent. For the first time since the end of World War II, there is a measurable and sustained shift away from the dollar as the world's default reserve asset.

No Easy Exit

De-dollarization is not a switch that can be flipped. The network effects that the Richmond Fed identified in 2022 are real. CIPS exists, but it handles only a tiny fraction of SWIFT's volume. ASEAN’s local currency settlement is a promising bilateral arrangement, but intra-ASEAN trade is only a quarter of the region's total trade. The dollar’s dominance is likely to persist for decades, even as its share declines gradually.

But that does not mean the currency game is a law of nature. It was designed by human beings at a specific moment in history, and it can be redesigned. The question is whether the incumbent beneficiaries — the United States, its allies, the financial institutions that profit from the dollar’s centrality — will allow a fairer system to emerge, or whether they will treat every reform as a threat to be neutralized.

For Malaysia, the immediate calculus is simpler. Every day that passes with a weak ringgit and global trade denominated in dollars is a day that we must work harder, earn less, and wait longer to achieve what Americans born into the reserve currency can take for granted. Our parents understood this intuitively. They saved in fixed deposits earning 3% while inflation quietly eroded 4%, because there was no easy currency-safe alternative. They watched the ringgit slide from RM2.50 to RM3.80 to RM4.70 over a single working lifetime. They adapted, as Malaysians always do. But adaptation is not a solution, and endurance is not a strategy. The system was not designed to be fair to us. It was designed to work for them. The question, for every Malaysian who has ever checked an exchange rate and felt that quiet, familiar sting, is what we intend to do about it.
